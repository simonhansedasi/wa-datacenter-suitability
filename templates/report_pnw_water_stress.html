Water Stress: Pacific Northwest vs. the Colorado River Compact States

Simon-Hans Edasi  /  Merascope

The Colorado River basin's water crisis is real and current -- a new federal plan released July 31, 2026 forces deep cuts on Arizona, California, and Nevada. This page checks what Merascope's water-stress data actually says about Washington and Oregon by comparison, using the real WRI Aqueduct watershed polygons -- not an administrative-boundary proxy.

Background

On July 31, 2026 the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation released a 10-year plan for managing the Colorado River after the states missed a February deadline to agree on their own terms. Lake Mead and Lake Powell's combined storage hit its lowest point in nearly 70 years -- about 12.7 million acre-feet in mid-July -- and the plan requires Arizona, California, and Nevada to cut use 16-20% through 2028 (CNN, July 31, 2026).

A peer-reviewed national siting study models Northwest water-scarcity footprint as lower than the Southwest's (Estrada et al., Environmental Research Letters, 2021). Columbia Riverkeeper and other advocates argue the Pacific Northwest is not actually water-abundant -- Columbia Basin water rights are already oversubscribed, and the region's hydropower system also has to protect salmon runs (Water Foundation / Columbia Riverkeeper Q&A, 2026).

Evidence & Methodology

Source: WRI Aqueduct 3.0 baseline water stress (withdrawal-to-supply ratio), read directly from Aqueduct's watershed polygons, each state clipped to its own real boundary. Shares are area-weighted (% of km², not polygon count). "Extremely high" is Aqueduct's own threshold: withdrawal-to-supply ratio above 80% (bws ≥ 4 on its 0–5 scale).

What this doesn't measure: this is local withdrawal-to-supply ratio, not Colorado River allocation risk -- Nevada and Utah both read low on this metric despite heavy Colorado River exposure, because Las Vegas draws ~90% of its water from Lake Mead while sitting in a Great Basin internal-drainage watershed that this metric scores separately. It's a long-run average, not current drought conditions. And it says nothing about water quality, which this map does not attempt to show.
