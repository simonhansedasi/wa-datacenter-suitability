Key Findings

1

Microsoft's own East Wenatchee campus sits inside a documented historical fire perimeter

EAT06/EAT09 (under construction) sits directly inside the 2005 Badger Road Fire's MTBS-mapped perimeter (3,993 acres). WHP's own hazard-potential model reads that same ground as "low" -- the agency-scored risk index does not know a real fire already burned there once. This is the only confirmed WA site of {{ dc_sites|length }} that sits inside any of the {{ n_perimeters }} MTBS-mapped fire perimeters since {{ year_min }}; every other site's own parcel reads "non-burnable" or "low" on WHP and sits outside every mapped perimeter -- expected, since data centers are mostly built on developed or irrigated agricultural land. EAT06/EAT09 is the exception, and it's real: land that has already burned once, not a model estimate.

2

Real fire history puts the rest of the Quincy/Malaga cluster closer than the hazard model alone suggests

WHP's fuel model reads agricultural and CRP grassland as non-burnable by definition -- the same land class that carried the 2020 Pearl Hill and Cold Springs fires (187,000-218,000 acres each) through this exact shrub-steppe interior. Checking distance to an actual MTBS burn perimeter instead of the model closes that gap: Microsoft's Malaga campus sits just 0.9 km from a real historical fire (1.4 km from WHP high/very-high hazard land); the Quincy cluster (Microsoft Quincy Campus, H5, Sabey) sits 3.8-4.3 km out; Amazon's proposed Wallula Gap site sits 7.9 km out. Every Seattle-metro site sits 47-50 km from any historical burn on record, in agreement with the model there. Two independent signals, and where they disagree, the real fire history is the one that already happened.

Evidence & Methodology

History: MTBS (Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity) burned-area boundary shapefile, clipped to Washington, real digitized fire perimeters back to 1984. Risk: USFS Wildfire Hazard Potential 2020, the classified 1-5 product (same "very low" to "very high" classes USFS publishes publicly), read directly rather than building a temperature-and-history composite for this page -- an agency-scored index folding in fuel, weather, topography, and historical occurrence already, not a number invented here.

What this doesn't measure: WHP's land-cover model reads developed and agricultural land as "non-burnable" by definition -- including Conservation Reserve Program grassland, which is exactly what carried the 2020 Pearl Hill and Cold Springs fires (187,000 - 218,000 acres each) through the same shrub-steppe interior this map shows as low-hazard on the fuel-model layer. This is a real, published limitation of the national WHP product, not something specific to this page -- USFS's own Wildfire Risk to Communities documentation confirms the default fuel models don't simulate fire spread across cropland or CRP grassland at all, and some states (Montana, for one) have built custom fuel models specifically to patch this gap for their own risk assessments; Washington hasn't, in the national product used here. The purple areas on the map above are exactly this overlap -- real MTBS burn perimeters sitting on ground WHP still calls non-burnable, made visible directly rather than left as a paragraph you have to take on faith. It's also why this page never blends WHP and MTBS into one invented score: two independent datasets that can disagree, and do disagree in an explicable, externally-documented way, are more trustworthy than one composite number that would have hidden this. That disagreement is patched, not papered over, by the "nearest burn (real)" column in the table above: it comes from actual MTBS fire perimeters, so it doesn't share WHP's blind spot -- it's the same column that caught EAT06/EAT09 sitting directly inside the 2005 Badger Road perimeter (Finding 1) and put Malaga just 0.9 km from real fire history (Finding 2), closer than the hazard model alone suggested. Treat the on-site WHP class as a fuel-model read, not a guarantee against grass-fire risk. Separately, the MTBS record's identification of individual named fires is not exhaustive by name -- large multi-fire complexes are sometimes mapped under their original component-fire names rather than a merged complex name, so a search for one specific historical fire by name may not surface it even though its burned ground is present in the dataset under a different name.
